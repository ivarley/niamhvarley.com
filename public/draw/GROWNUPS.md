# For grown-ups

Source for the grown-ups page. Edit this file and run `node build.js`.

## Read this bit first

Somewhere between nine and eleven, most children notice that their drawings don't look
real. A lot of them conclude they're not good at art, and stop — many of them for good.

That's the only thing this course is really designed around. Every choice on this site
follows from it. There are no streaks, no scores, no stars, nothing that turns a missed
week into a failure. Every lesson defines "done" as *the attempt happened*, never as the
drawing being any good. Nothing is locked, so she can do lesson 7 first if she wants.

You can undo the whole thing in about four sentences of well-meant feedback, so the
coaching section below matters more than the lessons do.

## Why these exercises

Children draw symbols, not observations. By about five, a child has a stored shape for
"eye", "tree", "horse", "dog" — and for years afterwards they draw the stored shape from
memory instead of looking at the thing in front of them. It's efficient, and it's why
children's drawings are so instantly recognisable as children's drawings.

The first half of any drawing course is a set of tricks for interrupting that habit:
drawing without looking at the paper, drawing the gaps instead of the object, measuring
before drawing. None of them are about talent. They're about looking.

The second half is about form — understanding that things are solid objects sitting in
light, not outlines. That's what makes a drawing read as three-dimensional.

Lesson 7 is the hinge. She draws a dog from memory, then builds one from a photo, and
puts them side by side. Don't explain it in advance. The comparison has to land on its
own or it doesn't land at all.

## The five things light does to a ball

This is the one piece of technical knowledge worth getting exactly right, because it's
usually the moment drawing starts to feel like magic. On a ball lit from one side:

- **Highlight** — the small bright spot where the lamp reflects.
- **Midtone** — the general lit surface, darkening as it curves away.
- **Core shadow** — a dark band on the shadow side. It is *not* at the outer edge. It
  sits just inside the rim.
- **Reflected light** — the rim beyond the core shadow, lighter again, because light
  bounces off the table back up onto the ball.
- **Cast shadow** — the shadow on the table. Darkest and sharpest where the ball touches,
  softer and lighter as it stretches away.

The two counter-intuitive ones — the darkest part isn't the rim, and the cast shadow
isn't uniform — are the difference between a sphere and a flat grey circle.

## Hatching

Endless Paper has no soft brushes, no blending and no layers. So all shading here is
hatching: parallel pen strokes, packed tight for dark, spread out for light, gone over at
a second angle for the darkest areas.

This isn't a workaround. It's how every etching and pen-and-ink drawing you've ever seen
was made. Two rules carry most of the weight:

- Stroke direction follows the surface. Strokes wrap around a can. They run straight
  across the flat face of a box.
- Strokes never cross a corner from one face to another. That's what makes an edge read
  as an edge.

Corners are the easiest thing to point at without commenting on whether the drawing is
any good.

## How to coach without wrecking it

**Never critique accuracy.** Not gently, not helpfully, not "that's great, but the arm's a
bit long". The moment drawing becomes a thing she can get wrong in front of you, the
stakes change and the fun starts leaking out.

**Ask instead of assess.** What were you going for? Which bit was hardest? What would you
change? Let her be the judge. If she asks you outright whether it's good, answer about one
specific thing she did — "the shadow underneath makes it look like it's really sitting on
the table" — not about the drawing as a whole.

**Praise the process, not the result.** Effort, sticking with it, and looking hard are
things she controls. Talent isn't, and praising it is how you get a child who won't
attempt anything difficult.

**Keep the scribbling alive.** These exercises are a side dish. If practice ever becomes
the price of being allowed to draw, she'll stop drawing. Most of her drawing time should
still be whatever she feels like, with no technique attached.

**Do the exercises yourself, badly, next to her.** This is the highest-leverage thing on
this page. A parent who is visibly worse at blind contour than their ten-year-old turns
the whole activity from assessment into company. Being a beginner is an asset here. Don't
hide it.

**Expect the frustration, and name it.** When the "this doesn't look real" moment arrives,
reassurance won't help. Information will: this happens to everybody, it means her eye has
got better faster than her hand, and the hand catches up with practice. That's true, and
it's the most useful thing you can tell her.

## Worth buying

**You Can Draw in 30 Days**, Mark Kistler. One short lesson a day, teaches 3D form and
shading straight away, pitched at exactly this energy level. The closest match to the arc
of these ten lessons, and probably the best single purchase.

**Drawing with Children**, Mona Brookes. Written for adults who can't draw, to teach
children who want to. This is the one that teaches *you* how to coach.

Later, when she starts asking for layers and blending and colour, Procreate on the iPad is
the standard next tool. There are months of good work before that's the thing holding her
back.

## What comes after these ten

Perspective, gesture drawing, faces and figure proportion, composition, and eventually
colour. Not built yet. Ten is enough to be getting on with.
