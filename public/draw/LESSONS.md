# The first ten lessons

Each lesson is about fifteen minutes. The arc runs in four phases, and the phases matter —
they should be visible on the home page, because the course is a sequence and the order
carries real information.

| Phase | Lessons | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Control | 1 | Making the line do what you meant |
| Looking | 2–5 | Interrupting the habit of drawing from memory |
| Building | 6–7 | Constructing objects out of simple solids |
| Light | 8–10 | Making flat things look solid |

## Page template

Every lesson page uses the same slots, in this order:

1. Phase and lesson number, title
2. **Skill** — one line

4. **Warm-up** — with a tappable timer

## Lesson 1 — Draw from your shoulder

**Phase:** Control
**Skill:** Confident lines
**You'll need:** Nothing but the app
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Zoom out until you have a big empty area. Pick the thinnest pen.

**The exercise:**

1. Put two dots on the canvas, far apart. Hover your pencil above the surface and swing it
   between them three or four times without touching down. Then commit: one fast stroke,
   straight through. Don't creep towards the second dot — aim at it and go.
2. Do that thirty times, in every direction.
3. Now ovals. Draw each one as a single loop that goes round three times without lifting
   the pencil. Twenty of them, all sizes.

**What to look for:** Fast lines come out straighter than slow ones. Slow careful lines
wobble. This is annoying and it's true, and it stays true forever.

**Done means:** Thirty lines and twenty ovals are on the canvas. Straightness is not the
point yet.

**In Endless Paper:** Keep the canvas zoomed out. The bigger the movement, the more your
arm does the work instead of your fingers.

**For the grown-up:** This is a physical habit, not an art skill — drawing from the
shoulder and elbow rather than the wrist. It's the single quickest thing that makes a
drawing look assured rather than tentative. Worth revisiting as a warm-up before any
later lesson. Do it yourself; adults are usually worse at it than children.

## Lesson 2 — Draw without looking

**Phase:** Looking
**Skill:** Actually seeing an edge
**You'll need:** Your other hand
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Ten fast lines from lesson 1.

**The exercise:**

1. Rest your other hand on the table in an interesting position.
2. Look at your hand. Put the pencil down on the canvas. Now do not look at the screen
   again until you're finished. Not once.
3. Move the pencil at exactly the speed your eye is crawling along the edge of your hand.
   Slow. Follow every knuckle and wrinkle.
4. Two minutes. Then look. Then do it twice more.

**What to look for:** It looks completely deranged. Ignore that. Look at the *detail* —
you recorded folds and bumps you have never drawn in your life, because for two minutes
you were looking at a hand instead of remembering one.

**Done means:** Three attempts, no peeking.

**In Endless Paper:** Do the three attempts side by side so you can see them together.

**For the grown-up:** Blind contour is the classic intervention for symbol drawing — the
habit of drawing the stored idea of a hand rather than the hand. It's also deliberately
impossible to do "well," which makes it safe. Laugh at yours first and loudest.

## Lesson 3 — Look five times more than you draw

**Phase:** Looking
**Skill:** Observation with the brakes on
**You'll need:** One shoe, or a sheet of paper crumpled into a ball
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Ten lines, ten ovals.

**The exercise:**

1. Put the shoe in front of you where you can see it properly.
2. Draw it. You're allowed to look at the screen this time — but keep to the ratio: about
   five seconds looking at the shoe for every one second looking at your drawing.
3. **House rule: no undo.** Not once, the whole lesson. A wrong line stays and you draw
   the right one next to it.

**What to look for:** Somewhere in the drawing there's a part you *looked at*, and a part
you *made up from memory*. You can usually tell which is which afterwards. The made-up
parts are the ones that came out fast.

**Done means:** One shoe drawn. Zero undos.

**In Endless Paper:** The undo button is right there and it's very tempting. That's why
this rule exists.

**For the grown-up:** Undo is the enemy of observation, because it lets you correct
towards the remembered symbol instead of looking again. Leaving wrong lines visible is
also how professional sketches actually look. Don't tidy hers.

## Lesson 4 — Draw the holes

**Phase:** Looking
**Skill:** Negative space
**You'll need:** A chair, or scissors, or a mug with a handle
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Ten lines.

**The exercise:**

1. Look at a chair from the side. Find the empty shapes: the gap between the legs, the
   slot between the seat and the back rest, the triangle under the arm.
2. Draw **only those empty shapes.** Draw no part of the chair. Fill each empty shape in
   solid.
3. Stop when you've done every gap. Now look at the whole thing.

**What to look for:** A chair appeared, and you never drew a chair. Empty shapes are much
easier to see accurately than objects, because you have no stored idea of what the gap
under a chair arm is supposed to look like.

**Done means:** Every gap in one object is filled in.

**In Endless Paper:** Try it once more zoomed way in on a small object, so the gaps are
huge on screen.

**For the grown-up:** This is the standard fix for proportion errors, and it works
instantly. If a later drawing looks off and neither of you can say why, come back here and
check the gaps.

## Lesson 5 — Measure with your pencil

**Phase:** Looking
**Skill:** Proportion
**You'll need:** Three objects on a table — say a mug, a book, an apple
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Ten ovals.

**The exercise:**

1. Sit still. You have to stay in the same spot the whole time or the measurements change.
2. Hold your pencil out at full arm's length, arm locked straight. Close one eye. Line the
   tip of the pencil up with the top of the mug and slide your thumb down to where the
   bottom of the mug is. That distance on the pencil is now **one mug**.
3. Keeping your thumb there, measure everything else in mugs. How many mugs wide is the
   book? How many mugs tall is the apple? Say the numbers out loud.
4. Now draw all three, using the numbers rather than what you think looks right.

**What to look for:** At least one of the numbers will be nothing like what you expected.
Things are wider, shorter, or further apart than they feel.

**Done means:** Three objects drawn, measured before drawn.

**In Endless Paper:** Use the lasso — if something ends up the wrong size, resize it
instead of redrawing it. Getting the *relationships* right is the whole job.

**For the grown-up:** Arm fully locked, or the unit changes between measurements. This is
called sighting and it's what life-drawing students spend the first month on.

## Lesson 6 — Everything is a box, a ball, a tube, or a cone

**Phase:** Building
**Skill:** Construction
**You'll need:** A look around the room
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Ten ovals — they're about to become very useful.

**The exercise:**

1. Draw the four solids in 3D, big: a box, a ball, a tube (cylinder), a cone. The trick
   for the tube and the cone is that the circular ends are drawn as ovals, never as
   straight lines or as perfect circles.
2. Now find eight real objects in the room and draw each one as nothing but those four
   solids stuck together. A lamp, a bottle, a dog, a chest of drawers, a person.
3. No detail. No faces, no patterns, no texture. Just the solids.

**What to look for:** Almost nothing in the world isn't made of these four things.

**Done means:** Four solids plus eight objects built from them.

**In Endless Paper:** Once you've drawn a good tube, lasso it and duplicate it. Real
artists reuse shapes constantly.

**For the grown-up:** This is the biggest single jump in making drawings look solid rather
than flat, and it's the skill Mark Kistler's book drills hardest. It will feel like a step
backwards to her because the drawings look cruder. It isn't.

## Lesson 7 — Draw the same dog twice

**Phase:** Building
**Skill:** Catching yourself drawing from memory
**You'll need:** A photo of a dog, or a real one
**Time:** 20 minutes

**Warm-up:** None. Go straight in.

**The exercise:**

1. **Without looking at anything**, draw a dog in sixty seconds. Whatever comes out.
2. Now put a photo of a real dog next to you and build a new one out of lesson 6's solids:
   a ball for the ribcage, a smaller ball for the hips, tubes for the legs and neck, a box
   for the muzzle. Get the solids in the right places and the right sizes before you draw
   a single hair or eye.
3. Then, and only then, draw the outline over the top of the solids.
4. Keep both drawings side by side. Don't delete the first one.

**What to look for:** The first dog is a *symbol* — the dog-shape you've had stored in
your head since you were about five, and you've been drawing it ever since without
noticing. The second one is an actual animal. Every subject you've ever drawn a lot has a
symbol like this hiding behind it. Horses. Eyes. Trees. Hands.

**Done means:** Two dogs, both kept.

**In Endless Paper:** Draw the solids in a light colour and the outline over the top in
black, so you can see the scaffolding underneath.

**For the grown-up:** This is the pivotal lesson of the whole course; the other nine are
support. Don't spoil it by explaining it in advance — the comparison has to land on its
own. Draw your own two dogs and notice that your symbol dog is from about 1987.

## Lesson 8 — Light on a ball

**Phase:** Light
**Skill:** Reading light
**You'll need:** A desk lamp, an egg or an orange or a plain ball, a dim room
**Time:** 20 minutes

**Warm-up (2 min):** Draw a ball. Yes, just an oval.

**The exercise:**

1. Put the ball on the table. Turn off the main light. Point the lamp at it from one side,
   fairly low down.
2. Before drawing anything, **find these five things with your finger:**
   - the **highlight** — the tiny bright spot where the lamp is reflecting
   - the **midtone** — the general lit side
   - the **core shadow** — a dark band on the dark side, and here's the surprise: it is
     *not* at the outer edge, it sits just inside it
   - the **reflected light** — the rim beyond the core shadow, slightly lighter again,
     because light is bouncing off the table back onto the ball
   - the **cast shadow** — the ball's shadow on the table, darkest right where the ball
     touches and softer as it stretches away
3. Now draw it, with hatching: parallel pen strokes, packed tight where it's dark, spread
   out where it's light, none at all on the highlight.

**What to look for:** The rim of the ball is not the darkest part. Get that one thing
right and a circle turns into a sphere.

**Done means:** One ball drawn with all five things attempted.

**In Endless Paper:** No smudging and no soft brush, so all shading is hatching. That's a
real technique, not a workaround — it's how ink drawings have been shaded for five hundred
years.

**For the grown-up:** This is usually the lesson where it clicks and drawing suddenly
feels like magic. Set the lamp up before you call her over; fiddling with lighting eats
the whole session. One lamp only — two light sources destroy the effect.

## Lesson 9 — Strokes that wrap

**Phase:** Light
**Skill:** Hatching that describes form
**You'll need:** The same lamp, a tin can and a box
**Time:** 15 minutes

**Warm-up (3 min):** Draw five squares in a row. Hatch them from nearly white to nearly
solid black, so they step evenly. That's a value scale, and it's the reference you'll use
from now on.

**The exercise:**

1. Light the tin can. Hatch it — but every stroke has to **curve around** the can, the way
   a stripe would if you painted one on it. Straight vertical strokes will make it look
   flat.
2. Light the box. Hatch each face separately, straight strokes, and give each face a
   different density. Never let strokes run across a corner from one face to the next.
3. For the darkest areas on both, go over your hatching a second time at a different angle
   — cross-hatching.

**What to look for:** The direction of your strokes tells the viewer the shape of the
surface, before they've even registered how dark it is.

**Done means:** A value scale, a hatched can, a hatched box.

**In Endless Paper:** Zoom in to lay strokes down accurately, then zoom out to check the
overall value. Hatching always looks wrong up close.

**For the grown-up:** Corners are the giveaway. If strokes run across an edge, the box
goes soft. Easy thing to point at without commenting on the drawing's quality.

## Lesson 10 — One real thing, all the way

**Phase:** Light
**Skill:** All of it at once
**You'll need:** One object you like. The lamp.
**Time:** 30 minutes

**Warm-up (3 min):** Lines, ovals, value scale.

**The exercise:**

Pick one object with some shape to it — a shoe, a teapot, a toy, a hand.

1. **Measure** it first, pencil at arm's length (lesson 5).
2. **Build** it out of solids, lightly (lesson 6).
3. **Look** and refine the outline over the solids, five-to-one, no undo (lessons 3, 7).
4. **Check** the gaps around it (lesson 4).
5. **Light** it, find the five things, hatch it with strokes that wrap (lessons 8, 9).

Take the full half hour. This is the first drawing in the course that's allowed to be
something you're pleased with.

**Then, when it's finished:** zoom right into the cast shadow of your drawing, until it
fills the screen. Start a whole new world inside it. That's yours, no rules.

**Done means:** One object taken through all five steps.

**In Endless Paper:** This drawing is the front door to whatever you build inside it. The
canvas doesn't end, so neither does this.

**For the grown-up:** Don't critique it. Ask what was hardest, or what she'd do
differently, and let her lead. Keep it — put it somewhere the next ten lessons can be
compared against.
