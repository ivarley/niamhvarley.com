# Notes for grown-ups

Source material for the "For grown-ups" page. Written for a parent with no art
background. Rewrite it in the site's voice — don't paste it verbatim.

## Why these exercises, in one paragraph

Children draw *symbols*, not observations. By about age five a child has a stored shape
for "eye," "tree," "horse," "dog," and for years afterwards they draw that stored shape
from memory rather than looking at the thing. It's efficient and it's why children's
drawings are so recognisable. The entire first half of any drawing course is tricks for
interrupting that habit: blind contour, negative space, sighting, drawing upside down.
None of them are about talent. They're about looking.

The second half is about form — understanding that objects are three-dimensional solids
sitting in light — which is what makes a drawing read as solid rather than flat.

## The five things light does to a ball

The one piece of technical knowledge worth getting exactly right, because it's the moment
drawings start to look real. On a sphere lit from one side:

- **Highlight** — the small bright reflection of the light source itself.
- **Midtone** — the general lit surface, getting gradually darker as it turns away.
- **Core shadow** — a dark band on the shadow side. **It is not at the outer edge.** It
  sits just inside the rim.
- **Reflected light** — the rim beyond the core shadow, lighter again, because light
  bounces off the table back up onto the ball.
- **Cast shadow** — the shadow thrown onto the table. Darkest and hardest-edged where the
  object touches the surface, softer and lighter as it stretches away.

The two counter-intuitive ones — that the darkest part isn't the rim, and that the cast
shadow isn't uniform — are what separate a sphere from a flat grey circle.

## Hatching

Since Endless Paper has no soft brushes or blending, all shading is done with hatching:
parallel pen strokes, packed tightly for dark, spread out for light, layered at a second
angle (cross-hatching) for the darkest areas. Two rules:

- Stroke direction should follow the curve of the surface. Strokes wrap around a cylinder;
  they run straight across a flat face.
- Strokes never cross a corner from one plane to another. That's what makes edges read as
  edges.

This is a real historical technique, not a compromise. Every etching and pen-and-ink
drawing you've ever seen works this way.

## How to coach without wrecking it

This is the part that actually determines whether she's still drawing at fourteen.

**Never critique accuracy.** Not gently, not helpfully, not "it's great, but the arm's a
bit long." The moment drawing becomes something she can get wrong in front of you, the
stakes change and the fun starts leaking out.

**Ask instead of assess.** "What were you going for?" "Which bit was hardest?" "What would
you change?" Let her be the judge. If she asks you directly whether it's good, answer
about the specific thing she did — "the shadow under it makes it look like it's actually
sitting on the table" — not about the drawing overall.

**Praise the process, not the result.** Effort, persistence, and looking hard are the
things she controls. Talent isn't, and praising it is how you produce a kid who won't
attempt anything difficult.

**Keep the scribbling alive.** These exercises are a side dish. If practice ever becomes
the price of being allowed to draw, she'll stop drawing. Most of her drawing time should
still be whatever she wants, with no technique attached.

**Do the exercises yourself, badly, next to her.** This is the highest-leverage thing on
this page. A parent who is visibly worse at blind contour than their child converts the
whole activity from assessment into company. Being a beginner is an asset here — don't
hide it.

**Expect the frustration and name it.** Somewhere around ages 9–11 nearly every child hits
the point of noticing their drawings don't look real, and a great many quit for life. If
it arrives, the useful response isn't reassurance, it's information: this happens to
everyone, it's a sign her eye has got better faster than her hand, and the hand catches up
with practice. That's true, and it's the most useful thing you can tell her.

## Worth buying

**You Can Draw in 30 Days**, Mark Kistler. One short lesson a day, teaches 3D form and
shading straight away, pitched exactly at a ten-year-old's energy. Probably the best
single purchase, and the closest match to the arc of these ten lessons.

**Drawing with Children**, Mona Brookes. Written for adults who can't draw, to teach
children who want to. This is the one that teaches *you* how to coach.

Later, when she's asking for layers and blending and colour: Procreate on iPad, one-off
purchase, the standard tool. But there are months of good work before that's the limiting
factor.
